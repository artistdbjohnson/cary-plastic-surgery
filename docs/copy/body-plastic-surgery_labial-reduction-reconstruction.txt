Labial reduction, commonly called labiaplasty, reshapes or reduces labial tissue. Labial reconstruction repairs or restores tissue changed by childbirth, injury, congenital differences, or previous surgery. Treatment most often involves the labia minora, although the labia majora may also be addressed when appropriate. The goal is a comfortable, proportionate result that respects sensation, function, and your individual anatomy.
Labia naturally vary widely in size, shape, color, and symmetry, and a difference in appearance does not by itself mean that treatment is needed. Some adults consider surgery because tissue twists, pulls, or chafes during exercise, fitted clothing, or intimacy. Others seek reconstruction after tissue has been stretched, torn, or altered. A private consultation is the right setting to discuss what is normal, what is causing symptoms, and whether surgery or a nonsurgical approach best fits your goals.

Technique Options
The best approach depends on the location of excess or damaged tissue, the natural edge and pigmentation you wish to preserve, and whether the goal is reduction, repair, or both.
Technique
Description
May Be Considered For
Trim Technique
Removes a measured amount of tissue along the outer edge, then closes the new border with fine absorbable sutures.
Excess tissue distributed along the labial edge or a preference to remove an irregular or darkened border.
Wedge Technique
Removes a small wedge from the thickest portion and brings the remaining edges together, preserving much of the natural outer border.
Central fullness when retaining the natural edge and pigmentation is a priority.
Reconstructive Repair
Repositions or repairs tissue using a plan based on the specific defect. More complex reconstruction may require local tissue rearrangement.
Tissue changes after childbirth, injury, congenital differences, previous surgery, or excessive prior reduction.
No single technique is best for every patient. Dr. Hanna will explain the proposed incision pattern, the tissue that would remain, and the tradeoffs before you decide.
Labial Surgery, Step-by-Step
Labial reduction is usually an outpatient procedure. A straightforward reduction often takes about one to two hours; reconstruction may take longer depending on complexity. Your individual plan may include:
Planning:
Confirm the treatment area and mark the amount of tissue to be reshaped while you are awake.
Anesthesia:
Use local anesthesia with sedation or general anesthesia based on the planned procedure and your needs.
Reduction or Repair:
Carefully remove, reshape, or reposition tissue using the selected technique.
Closure:
Close the incisions with fine, usually absorbable sutures and provide detailed wound-care instructions.
Recovery & Aftercare
Swelling, bruising, tenderness, and minor spotting are expected early in recovery. Many patients return to nonstrenuous work after several days to two weeks, depending on their procedure and job. Wear loose clothing, keep the area clean as directed, and use cold packs only according to your postoperative instructions.
Strenuous exercise, cycling, swimming, tampon use, and sexual activity are generally restricted for four to six weeks or until Dr. Hanna clears you. Most swelling improves by about six weeks, but subtle swelling can take four to six months to resolve. Your follow-up plan takes priority over any general timeline.
Risks & Complications
Labial surgery has risks, and high-quality long-term evidence for elective cosmetic genital procedures is limited. Possible complications include bleeding or hematoma, infection, delayed healing or wound separation, visible or tender scarring, asymmetry, removing too much or too little tissue, persistent pain, pain with intimacy, altered or reduced sensation, and the need for revision surgery. Anesthesia and blood-clot risks also apply.
Careful examination, conservative planning, smoking cessation, and following postoperative restrictions can reduce risk but cannot eliminate it. Dr. Hanna will review the risks that apply to your anatomy and proposed procedure during informed consent.

Frequently Asked Questions
What is the difference between labial reduction and reconstruction?
Reduction removes and reshapes a measured amount of excess tissue. Reconstruction repairs or restores tissue changed by childbirth, injury, congenital differences, previous surgery, or excessive prior reduction. Some procedures involve both.
How do I know whether my anatomy is normal?
Labia have a very wide normal range of size, shape, color, and symmetry. There is no single ideal measurement. A consultation can determine whether your concern is a normal variation, whether tissue is causing symptoms, and whether treatment is appropriate.
Will there be visible scars?
Incisions are placed within natural folds and scars often soften over time, but no surgeon can promise an invisible scar. Scar location and length depend on the technique and amount of repair.
Will surgery change sensation or sexual function?
The plan is designed to preserve healthy tissue and function, but temporary or lasting sensation changes and pain with intimacy are recognized risks. Evidence does not support promising improved sexual function from elective cosmetic surgery.
When can I exercise or resume sexual activity?
Most patients avoid strenuous exercise, cycling, swimming, tampon use, and sexual activity for about four to six weeks. Resume these activities only after Dr. Hanna examines your healing and clears you.
Can pregnancy or childbirth affect the result?
Yes. Pregnancy, vaginal delivery, aging, and major weight changes may stretch or alter tissue after surgery. If pregnancy is planned soon, Dr. Hanna may recommend postponing elective treatment.
Does insurance cover labial surgery?
Cosmetic reduction is usually not covered. A reconstructive procedure or surgery for documented persistent symptoms may be considered by some plans, but coverage and preauthorization requirements vary.
